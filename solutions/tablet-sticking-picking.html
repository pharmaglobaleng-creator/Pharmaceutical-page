What causes tablet sticking and picking?

Tablet sticking and picking can result from interacting formulation, process, tooling, geometry, surface, coating, cleaning, and environmental variables. Sticking usually affects a broader punch-face area, while picking is localized around engraving, logos, score lines, character islands, and other recessed features. A strong investigation classifies the defect, documents when and where it appears, and evaluates the complete compression system before selecting corrective action.

Engineering Facts Library

Tablet Sticking & Picking: Detailed Answers

Each fact below includes a direct answer and a practical engineering action. These facts are educational and should be reviewed against the manufacturer’s product, tooling, process, quality, safety, and validation requirements.

1

Tablet sticking and tablet picking are different defects.

Fact: Tablet sticking and tablet picking are different defects.

Answer: Tablet sticking usually affects a broader punch-face area, while picking is localized around lettering, logos, score lines, character islands, or other recessed features. The distinction matters because broad adhesion often points toward formulation, moisture, lubrication, heat, or overall surface condition, while localized picking places more emphasis on engraving geometry and local surface behavior.
Engineering action

Classify the visible defect before changing the process or tooling.

2

Moisture can increase tablet sticking.

Fact: Moisture can increase tablet sticking.

Answer: Excess residual moisture, high room humidity, hygroscopic ingredients, or moisture uptake during storage may increase tackiness and adhesion. The effect depends on formulation chemistry, granulation, drying, and compression temperature, so moisture should be evaluated with batch history rather than judged from one number alone.
Engineering action

Compare sticking onset with drying data, storage time, and room conditions.

3

Over-dry granulation can also affect release.

Fact: Over-dry granulation can also affect release.

Answer: Very low moisture may change compactability, lubrication behavior, particle fracture, electrostatic behavior, and interparticle bonding. A formulation can therefore become difficult to release when it is too wet or too dry. The optimum moisture range is application-specific.
Engineering action

Avoid assuming that lower moisture always means lower sticking.

4

Binder concentration influences adhesion.

Fact: Binder concentration influences adhesion.

Answer: Binders increase cohesion so tablets can form, but the same chemistry may also increase adhesive interaction with punch faces. Binder type, level, distribution, granulation method, drying, and compression conditions all influence whether the binder contributes to sticking.
Engineering action

Review binder changes together with granulation and drying history.

5

Magnesium stearate mixing time matters.

Fact: Magnesium stearate mixing time matters.

Answer: Magnesium stearate level and blending time affect particle coverage, die-wall lubrication, ejection, tablet strength, dissolution-related properties, and surface release. Under-blending and over-blending can create different problems, so more mixing is not automatically better.
Engineering action

Document lubricant level, order of addition, and actual blend time.

6

Glidants and anti-adherents are formulation tools, not universal fixes.

Fact: Glidants and anti-adherents are formulation tools, not universal fixes.

Answer: Colloidal silicon dioxide and other glidants or anti-adherents may improve flow or reduce adhesion in some formulations, but they cannot correct damaged punches, poor engraving geometry, excessive moisture, heat, or an unsuitable process window.
Engineering action

Evaluate formulation aids within the complete system.

7

Powder rheology affects die fill and tooling contact.

Fact: Powder rheology affects die fill and tooling contact.

Answer: Flowability, cohesiveness, particle size distribution, fines, granule strength, density, and deformation behavior influence how material enters the die, redistributes under compression, and separates from tooling surfaces.
Engineering action

Include flow and particle information in the troubleshooting record.

8

Fines can increase product retention.

Fact: Fines can increase product retention.

Answer: A high proportion of fine particles can increase surface area, cohesiveness, dusting, and the ability of material to pack into scratches, pits, lettering, and score lines. Fines may also respond differently to lubrication and moisture.
Engineering action

Compare sieve data and fines content across good and bad batches.

9

Hygroscopic ingredients can change during the run.

Fact: Hygroscopic ingredients can change during the run.

Answer: Hygroscopic materials may absorb moisture from the environment after blending or during press operation. Sticking can therefore begin only after exposure time, even when the initial batch condition appears acceptable.
Engineering action

Track elapsed exposure time before compression and before defects appear.

10

Compression force can change sticking behavior.

Fact: Compression force can change sticking behavior.

Answer: Higher compression force may increase contact stress, deformation, heat, and the area of intimate contact between formulation and tooling. In some formulations it can worsen sticking; in others, insufficient consolidation can contribute to poor release or weak surfaces.
Engineering action

Evaluate force with tablet quality, not in isolation.

11

Dwell time is not the same as compression force.

Fact: Dwell time is not the same as compression force.

Answer: Dwell time is the duration under peak compression, while force is the applied load. Both affect consolidation and heat, but they influence materials differently. Press speed, punch head geometry, and machine design also affect effective dwell time.
Engineering action

Record both force and speed when comparing trials.

12

Press speed can intensify sticking.

Fact: Press speed can intensify sticking.

Answer: Higher speed reduces available dwell time and may alter die fill, air release, heat accumulation, and the interval between punch contacts. It can also accelerate the rate at which a small amount of residue becomes a production-stopping buildup.
Engineering action

Compare defect onset at controlled speed steps.

13

Heat can soften susceptible formulations.

Fact: Heat can soften susceptible formulations.

Answer: Frictional and process heat may soften binders, waxy ingredients, or other thermally sensitive components. Tooling and press surfaces can warm during a run, which explains why sticking may develop after an initially acceptable startup.
Engineering action

Record temperature and time-to-defect, not only room conditions.

14

Punch-face scratches can become retention sites.

Fact: Punch-face scratches can become retention sites.

Answer: Scratches interrupt the intended surface and may trap fines, binder-rich material, or cleaning residue. Once material anchors in the defect, subsequent tablets can increase buildup.
Engineering action

Inspect under controlled lighting and magnification.

15

Corrosion pits can worsen sticking and cleaning.

Fact: Corrosion pits can worsen sticking and cleaning.

Answer: Pitting creates localized cavities that retain product and are difficult to clean consistently. Corrosion may also undermine coatings and expand under repeated exposure.
Engineering action

Treat recurring corrosion as both a surface and process-control issue.

16

Surface roughness is not described by Ra alone.

Fact: Surface roughness is not described by Ra alone.

Answer: Ra is an average. It does not fully describe isolated scratches, pits, waviness, directionality, peak shape, coating defects, or local roughness around engraving. Two punches with similar Ra values can behave differently in production.
Engineering action

Use roughness data together with visual and functional inspection.

17

The lowest possible Ra is not always the best target.

Fact: The lowest possible Ra is not always the best target.

Answer: An extremely low average roughness may not improve release if chemistry, geometry, localized defects, or coating condition dominate. Over-polishing can also remove material from critical features.
Engineering action

Define a functional surface objective, not only a cosmetic one.

18

Polishing must preserve punch geometry.

Fact: Polishing must preserve punch geometry.

Answer: Controlled polishing should protect cup profile, land, edges, score lines, embossing, and dimensional relationships. Aggressive or uneven polishing can change tablet appearance and tooling function.
Engineering action

Measure and document critical features before and after service.

19

Improper polishing can damage engraving.

Fact: Improper polishing can damage engraving.

Answer: Rounded character edges, reduced depth, weakened islands, and altered logos can result from uncontrolled polishing. These changes may reduce legibility and can even create new picking sites.
Engineering action

Engraving requires its own protection and inspection plan.

20

Cup depth affects material flow and release.

Fact: Cup depth affects material flow and release.

Answer: Deep or complex cup geometry can increase local material movement, contact pressure, and separation difficulty. Compound radii and transitions may concentrate stress or create areas where formulation is more likely to remain.
Engineering action

Evaluate cup design together with formulation behavior.

21

Score lines can become picking locations.

Fact: Score lines can become picking locations.

Answer: Score lines create recessed geometry and sharp transitions that may retain product. Depth, width, edge shape, intersection with the cup, and local surface finish all influence performance.
Engineering action

Map the exact location of residue within the score feature.

22

Character islands are high-risk features.

Fact: Character islands are high-risk features.

Answer: Letters and numbers with enclosed islands can produce narrow walls and small unsupported features. Material may pack around these areas, and the islands may be vulnerable to wear or damage.
Engineering action

Review font geometry during tablet design, not only after picking appears.

23

Engraving depth influences picking risk.

Fact: Engraving depth influences picking risk.

Answer: Deeper engraving can improve visual definition but may increase the distance material must release from a recessed feature. Excessive depth can also create narrow cavities that are harder to polish and clean.
Engineering action

Balance legibility, regulatory needs, strength, and manufacturability.

24

Sharp internal corners can retain product.

Fact: Sharp internal corners can retain product.

Answer: Sharp corners in logos and characters create zones where material flow is restricted and separation is difficult. Rounded or optimized transitions may reduce retention without compromising identity.
Engineering action

Use manufacturability review before approving final artwork.

25

Pre-picking features require controlled design.

Fact: Pre-picking features require controlled design.

Answer: Relief or pre-picking features can improve local release, but they must be engineered around the specific character, cup, tablet design, strength, and legibility requirements.
Engineering action

Avoid generic relief changes applied without validation.

26

Upper and lower punches may behave differently.

Fact: Upper and lower punches may behave differently.

Answer: Temperature, gravity, powder movement, punch-face geometry, venting, and contact history can make sticking more severe on one punch set than the other.
Engineering action

Record whether buildup occurs on upper, lower, or both punches.

27

Station-specific defects can indicate localized tooling problems.

Fact: Station-specific defects can indicate localized tooling problems.

Answer: When only certain stations show sticking or picking, localized punch damage, coating failure, alignment, or press condition may be more likely than a purely formulation-wide cause.
Engineering action

Map defects by station before mixing tools or cleaning the set.

28

Filming can precede severe sticking.

Fact: Filming can precede severe sticking.

Answer: A light haze or progressive residue layer may be an early warning. Filming can change friction and provide a base for larger deposits.
Engineering action

Photograph the punch face at planned intervals during the run.

29

Cleaning residue can contribute to adhesion.

Fact: Cleaning residue can contribute to adhesion.

Answer: Detergent, solvent, polish, oil, or incomplete rinse residue can change surface chemistry and attract formulation material. Cleaning history should be part of the investigation.
Engineering action

Use documented, compatible cleaning and drying procedures.

30

Abrasive cleaning can damage functional surfaces.

Fact: Abrasive cleaning can damage functional surfaces.

Answer: Abrasive pads, compounds, or uncontrolled manual methods can scratch punch faces, round engraving, remove coating, and produce uneven surface condition.
Engineering action

Choose cleaning tools and chemistry based on the surface system.

31

Coatings are application-specific.

Fact: Coatings are application-specific.

Answer: CrN, DLC, TiN, TiCN, and other PVD systems have different properties. Their performance depends on the substrate, preparation, formulation, geometry, edges, cleaning, wear, impact, corrosion, and operating environment.
Engineering action

Select coatings from the failure mechanism, not from color or hardness alone.

32

CrN is not a universal anti-stick solution.

Fact: CrN is not a universal anti-stick solution.

Answer: Chromium nitride may provide useful wear, corrosion, and surface-performance characteristics in suitable applications, but it cannot correct all moisture, binder, geometry, heat, or process-related causes.
Engineering action

Evaluate CrN after defining the root cause.

33

DLC is a coating family, not one single material.

Fact: DLC is a coating family, not one single material.

Answer: Different diamond-like carbon systems can vary in hardness, friction, hydrogen content, thickness, adhesion, temperature tolerance, and application fit.
Engineering action

Identify the actual DLC system before comparing performance.

34

TiN may improve wear without solving sticking.

Fact: TiN may improve wear without solving sticking.

Answer: Titanium nitride is a hard PVD coating that may support wear resistance, but a hard surface alone does not eliminate adhesive interactions driven by formulation or geometry.
Engineering action

Separate wear objectives from adhesion objectives.

35

TiCN has a distinct hardness and friction profile.

Fact: TiCN has a distinct hardness and friction profile.

Answer: Titanium carbonitride differs from TiN, CrN, and DLC. Its selection should be based on the actual wear and friction requirements, not on a generic ranking.
Engineering action

Compare the complete coating-substrate system.

36

Coating performance depends on substrate preparation.

Fact: Coating performance depends on substrate preparation.

Answer: A coating follows and depends on the underlying surface. Scratches, poor edge condition, contamination, unsuitable roughness, corrosion, or weak preparation can limit adhesion and service life.
Engineering action

Inspect and prepare the substrate before coating.

37

Coating edges and transitions require attention.

Fact: Coating edges and transitions require attention.

Answer: Sharp edges, thin sections, engraving islands, score lines, and high-impact areas can be difficult coating locations. Local stress may contribute to early damage.
Engineering action

Review geometry before choosing coating thickness and process.

38

A coating can fail because of cleaning.

Fact: A coating can fail because of cleaning.

Answer: Incompatible chemistry, abrasive cleaning, ultrasonic conditions, impact, or poor handling may damage the coating or expose the substrate.
Engineering action

Include cleaning compatibility in coating selection.

39

Restoration can be better than replacement in suitable cases.

Fact: Restoration can be better than replacement in suitable cases.

Answer: Serviceable tooling with recoverable scratches, corrosion, buildup, or surface damage may retain useful life when critical dimensions, geometry, engraving, and integrity remain acceptable.
Engineering action

Use inspection criteria rather than appearance alone.

40

Replacement is necessary when integrity is lost.

Fact: Replacement is necessary when integrity is lost.

Answer: Cracks, severe dimensional loss, chipped critical edges, unsafe structural damage, or geometry that cannot be preserved are replacement conditions, not polishing problems.
Engineering action

Define non-restorable limits before work begins.

41

Photographs support preliminary evaluation.

Fact: Photographs support preliminary evaluation.

Answer: Clear punch-face, side-view, engraving, coating, and tablet-defect photographs can reveal patterns and support triage. They do not always replace physical inspection.
Engineering action

Use consistent lighting, scale, and multiple angles.

42

A good investigation records when the defect begins.

Fact: A good investigation records when the defect begins.

Answer: Immediate sticking suggests a different mechanism from buildup that starts after heat, humidity exposure, or a long run. Timing is a critical diagnostic clue.
Engineering action

Record tablets produced or elapsed minutes before onset.

43

Controlled trials should change one variable at a time.

Fact: Controlled trials should change one variable at a time.

Answer: Changing force, speed, lubrication, moisture, tooling, and cleaning together makes it difficult to identify the actual cause. Controlled trials produce stronger evidence.
Engineering action

Document baseline, change, outcome, and repeatability.

44

The visible defect may be a symptom of a broader system problem.

Fact: The visible defect may be a symptom of a broader system problem.

Answer: Sticking, picking, wear, corrosion, or coating damage can reflect upstream granulation, storage, cleaning, press condition, material selection, or tablet design issues.
Engineering action

Do not limit the investigation to the punch face.

45

Application-specific recommendations are stronger than generic fixes.

Fact: Application-specific recommendations are stronger than generic fixes.

Answer: The same corrective action can help one formulation and fail another. Recommendations should be based on the actual product, tooling, press, environment, defect pattern, and production objectives.
Engineering action

Build the recommendation from evidence.

Frequently Asked Questions

Tablet Sticking & Picking FAQ

What is tablet sticking?

Tablet sticking is adhesion across a broader punch-face area during compression. It can cause buildup, rough tablet surfaces, interrupted release, and production stops.

What is tablet picking?

Tablet picking is localized adhesion, often around lettering, logos, score lines, character islands, or recessed punch-face features.

What is the difference between sticking and picking?

Sticking usually affects a wider surface area, while picking is concentrated around local geometry.

Can punch polishing reduce sticking?

It may help when scratches, roughness, corrosion, or product-retention sites contribute, but it may not solve formulation or process causes.

Does every sticky formulation need a coating?

No. The better response may be formulation review, moisture control, lubrication adjustment, process changes, polishing, restoration, engraving optimization, or coating evaluation.

Which coating is best for sticking?

There is no universal best coating. CrN, DLC, TiN, TiCN, and other PVD coatings must be matched to the application.

Can moisture cause sticking?

Yes. Excess moisture, hygroscopic behavior, and humidity exposure can increase tackiness, but over-dry granulation can also affect release.

Can compression force cause sticking?

Force can influence contact stress, deformation, heat, and release behavior. The effect depends on the formulation.

Can press speed cause sticking?

Higher speed can change dwell time, heat, die fill, and the rate at which residue accumulates.

Can engraving cause picking?

Engraving depth, narrow spacing, islands, sharp internal corners, rough walls, and score lines can increase localized retention.

Can damaged coated punches be restored?

Possibly, after the coating, substrate, dimensions, edges, damage mechanism, and reprocessing route are evaluated.

What information should be submitted for evaluation?

Provide clear tooling and tablet photos, defect location, timing, tooling material and coating, cleaning methods, press conditions, formulation observations, and previous trials.

Can PharmaGlobalEng evaluate international projects?

Yes. Preliminary technical review and project coordination can be performed for manufacturers worldwide, subject to scope, shipping, documentation, customs, and service availability.

How often should tablet tooling be inspected?

Inspection frequency should reflect production volume, formulation abrasiveness, cleaning, coating history, prior defects, and risk.

When should punches be replaced?

Replacement may be required when cracks, severe dimensional loss, critical edge damage, unsafe structural problems, or unrecoverable geometry are present.
