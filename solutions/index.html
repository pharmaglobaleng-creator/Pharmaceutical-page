Start with where and when the defect appears

The same rejected tablet can show several symptoms, so record the first observable change. Does material remain on the punch face? Does the tablet separate as it leaves the die, or only after transfer? Does the weight move before breaking strength changes? A useful investigation distinguishes the time a defect is created from the time it is noticed. Keep representative acceptable and affected tablets labeled with their collection point and time.

Use the guides below to narrow the investigation. The descriptions are routes for gathering evidence, not diagnoses from appearance alone. If the defect cannot yet be classified, begin with the compression defects guide. Record the product and batch, press identity, tooling set, station when traceable, speed, available force records, recent changes, and how samples were handled. An incomplete but clearly labeled record is more useful than a confident explanation unsupported by observations.

Match the visible symptom to a focused investigation

For material adhering broadly to a punch face, start with tablet sticking. When the defect follows lettering, a logo, or another local feature, review tablet picking. The combined sticking and picking guide helps compare these patterns without treating them as interchangeable. Photograph the tablet and corresponding tool surface before evidence is lost through cleaning, while following the site’s product-handling procedures.

For tablet separation, compare capping, lamination, and chipping. Record the shape and location of the missing or separated portion and whether the symptom is present at the press discharge. A defect observed only after a transfer, deduster, or later operation needs a different comparison from one already visible at ejection. Retaining fragments with the relevant sample can help preserve that distinction.

Difficult ejection or tablet sidewall marks belong in the binding investigation. Do not automatically classify them as punch-face sticking. The tablet, die bore, tooling history, and available ejection observations need to be considered together. Avoid changing several possible causes at once, because an apparent improvement then provides little evidence about which change mattered.

Keep process variation separate from tool damage

Weight variation examines variation in measured tablet mass, including filling and control evidence and any observed material loss or damage. Hardness optimization asks how the formed tablet responds to a defined strength test alongside its other required properties. Review the sampling method before interpreting either trend. A change in test orientation, collection timing, or sample handling can make unlike results appear comparable.

Dwell time and compression force are related process considerations, but they are not interchangeable settings. A trial should define the variable being evaluated, the conditions being held, the response being measured, and the acceptance limits supplied by the responsible team. Record unsuccessful trials as well as successful ones so the next review does not repeat an already-tested assumption.

Visible damage on the tools starts another branch of the investigation. Use punch wear for loss or change of a working feature, corrosion for suspected chemical attack, and cracking for suspected loss of structural integrity. A shiny surface is not proof that any of these conditions has been resolved. Inspection and the applicable specification determine what still needs attention.

Build a comparison that can support a corrective action

Select an unaffected comparison that is as similar as practical to the affected condition. Examples include another station from the same run, an earlier sample before the defect appeared, or the documented condition before maintenance. Explain the differences that remain between the samples. Comparing unrelated products or undocumented tool sets can produce an attractive explanation while leaving the original question unanswered.

Write down the proposed cause and the observation that would support or weaken it. If the working theory concerns one station, retain station identity. If it concerns a cleaning change, collect the previous and current cleaning records. If it concerns a tool surface, document its condition before and after the proposed work. This connects the selected service to a testable question rather than a general instruction to improve performance.

Use inspection to establish condition, failure analysis to investigate a failure mechanism, or press troubleshooting when the system interaction remains unclear. Before changing production settings or returning repaired tooling, the responsible manufacturer should define the evaluation and approval requirements. To request support, send the observed defect and the decision you need to make, together with the available evidence.

Use a defect record that preserves the useful differences

The description “more rejects” combines several possible outcomes. Record the defect category, the number examined, the number affected, and the point in the process where the sample was taken. Preserve upper-face and lower-face observations when they differ. If a tablet has both a missing edge and a visible layer separation, record both features rather than choosing whichever label seems more familiar. Consistent categories make a later comparison more meaningful.

Keep measurements connected to the same sampling period. A hardness result collected after an adjustment should not silently be paired with a weight result from before it. If station information is unavailable, say so; do not reconstruct it from a pattern that the sampling method cannot establish. These limits do not make the evidence useless. They show which conclusions remain possible and which would require a different observation method.

Retain a record of apparently acceptable periods as well as failures. The last known acceptable condition can establish which changes preceded the symptom. A final photograph of the worst tablet cannot provide that chronology on its own. Record cleaning stops, component substitutions, material replenishment, and the time of each trial so the next reviewer can understand the sequence.

Two examples of choosing the next useful check

Suppose tablets appear intact at the earliest approved collection point but show edge loss after a transfer. The next comparison should focus on the transition where damage first appears, using the same defect definition before and after it. Replacing the entire punch set would not test that observation directly. The chipping guide then helps separate the condition of the tablet from the handling it receives. This is an illustrative investigation route, not a diagnosis of a particular machine.

In a second example, tablet mass drifts during a run while average breaking force also changes. The two measurements should be reviewed together with the filling and control records. A force adjustment might alter the strength result without resolving the mass trend. Start by checking the timing, sample identity, and actual machine control behavior, then use the weight and hardness guides for their separate questions. Record any factors changed together so the comparison does not claim to isolate a cause it never tested.

The value of either example is the connection between a specific observation and a next check. A useful investigation does not need an elaborate list of every possible cause. It needs enough evidence to choose the next discriminating comparison and to explain why that comparison addresses the reported problem.

Know when the work needs a different specialist or scope

A tooling photograph cannot establish formulation behavior, an instrument calibration result, or every aspect of press condition. When the available evidence points beyond the submitted tools, identify the additional information or specialist review required. Keep that boundary visible in the enquiry. A preliminary tooling assessment should not be interpreted as approval of an entire manufacturing process.

For an unresolved structural defect, preserve the tooling and follow the responsible team’s isolation and investigation procedures. For a proposed tablet or tooling design change, identify the applicable drawings and approvals before altering a feature. For a product-performance concern, involve the team responsible for the relevant specifications and testing. The selected corrective action should address both the technical finding and the authority required to implement it.

25 practical questions and answers

Quick answers to the questions covered in this guide. Use the detailed sections above for the evidence and context behind each answer.

01

What should a tablet-defect investigation identify first?

Record what is observed and where and when it first appears, before assigning a cause.

02

How does sticking differ from picking?

Sticking involves broader adhesion to the punch face; picking is localized around features such as lettering or a logo.

03

What distinguishes tablet binding from punch-face sticking?

Binding concerns die-wall resistance and release during ejection; punch-face sticking concerns material retained on the tablet-forming face.

04

How does capping differ from lamination?

Capping involves separation of a crown, while lamination involves splitting within the tablet body into layers.

05

What is tablet chipping?

Chipping is localized loss of edge material and should be recorded separately from crown separation or internal layering.

06

Why record the first confirmed point of damage?

It helps distinguish a defect already present at ejection from damage introduced during transfer or a later operation.

07

What is the first route when the defect is unclear?

Use the broad compression-defects guide to classify observations and identify the next evidence needed.

08

Why keep acceptable comparison tablets?

They provide a reference from a known sampling period for judging the affected condition.

09

What should accompany a defect count?

Record the number examined, number affected, category, collection point, and sampling period.

10

Should different defects be combined into one reject figure?

Keep separate categories when mechanisms may differ; one combined number can conceal a change from one defect to another.

11

What does a weight-variation investigation examine?

It examines variation in measured tablet mass, using sampling, filling and control records while considering any observed material loss or damage.

12

What does a hardness investigation need besides a strength result?

It needs the method, sample context, mass, dimensions, and other applicable product requirements.

13

Are dwell time and compression force interchangeable?

No. They describe different aspects of compression and must be defined separately in a trial.

14

Why can a speed comparison be difficult to interpret?

A speed change can affect more than compression timing, so other changing conditions must be recorded.

15

How should wear and corrosion be distinguished in a tooling enquiry?

Describe the actual location and condition before labeling the mechanism; loss of a feature and suspected chemical attack require different evidence.

16

Does a shiny tool prove damage has been resolved?

No. Appearance does not establish dimensional conformity or structural integrity.

17

What makes a useful unaffected comparison?

It is as similar as practical to the affected condition, with remaining differences explicitly recorded.

18

Why preserve station information when available?

It connects recurring observations with specific tools and associated press features.

19

Can station identity be guessed from an untraceable sample?

No. If the sampling method does not preserve station identity, record that limitation instead of reconstructing it by assumption.

20

What should a trial question specify?

State the proposed cause, the comparison being made, the measured response, and the evidence that would support or weaken the explanation.

21

What happens when several factors change together?

The result evaluates the combined change and cannot by itself establish which single factor caused the outcome.

22

Why retain unsuccessful trial records?

They show what was tested, under which conditions, and help avoid repeating an unsupported approach.

23

When should investigation extend beyond the tooling?

When the evidence points to product behavior, instrumentation, machine condition, or another question that submitted tooling cannot establish.

24

Does a preliminary tooling review approve an entire process?

No. Its scope and evidence limits must remain distinct from the manufacturer’s production-approval requirements.

25

What should a support request ask for?

Describe the symptom, provide relevant evidence, and state the decision or missing comparison that the review needs to address.
