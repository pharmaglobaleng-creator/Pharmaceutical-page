Tablet Lamination: Investigate the Separation Before Changing the Tooling

Browse 25 quick answers

When tablets split into layers, keep the fracture evidence and the process history together. The next step should follow what happened during the run—not an automatic increase in compression force or an order for replacement punches.

Describe the separation precisely

Lamination is splitting within the tablet body into layers. Capping involves separation of a crown. Use the visible pattern to describe the defect before assigning a cause.[1]

Photograph the tablet from the side as well as from the faces. Record whether the split is complete, whether several planes are visible and whether the pattern repeats across samples. Preserve the sample orientation and the point at which it was collected.

Separate observations from interpretation. “A horizontal split became visible after transfer” is an observation. “The die trapped air” is a proposed explanation that still needs evidence. Keeping those statements separate makes the investigation easier to review and prevents an early assumption from deciding the repair.

For a deliberately multilayer tablet, also identify whether the separation is at a designed layer interface. That question belongs in the product-specific investigation rather than being treated as identical to a split in a single-layer compact.

Do not treat every laminated tablet as the same mechanism

Air-entrapment hypothesis. A 2024 study used in-die elastic-recovery measurements to optimize precompression for a model blend with air-entrapment-related lamination.[2] This supports investigating the precompression stage when the evidence fits; it is not a universal pressure setting for production.

Unloading hypothesis. Research on a compaction simulator found that changing unloading conditions changed the occurrence of capping and lamination.[3] Keep information about the press and the compression cycle available to the engineer reviewing the problem.

Layer-interface hypothesis. In a study of one bilayer formulation, internal cracking and ejection-related stresses were investigated together.[4] The appropriate assessment depends on the product; do not transfer a bilayer result into a universal explanation for all tablets.

The purpose of identifying a hypothesis is to decide what observation would support or challenge it. Avoid collecting a large file of unrelated readings without stating which question each one helps answer.

Compare the affected run with a suitable baseline

Use a successful run of the same product and tooling design where available. Record differences openly when the baseline is not directly comparable. The following is a suggested investigation worksheet, not a list of mandatory process settings.

Keep the raw observations where possible, not only a conclusion such as “pressure normal.” Identify the instrument or record used and any missing data. A comparison is weaker when a setting was recalled later rather than captured during the run.

Review die geometry without guessing a taper or clearance

A 2026 study examined die taper, punch–die clearance and compaction position using two formulations. The effects differed between those formulations.[5] That is a useful warning against treating a tapered die as an automatic answer.

Send the existing die specification and approved punch drawings for engineering review. Record whether the problem started with a tooling change, whether the nominal design is the same and whether service work has altered the set’s condition. Keep dimensional checks traceable to the actual tools.

Do not machine a die, enlarge a clearance or change punch penetration from a general web recommendation. Any proposal must address compatibility, the required geometry, equipment limits and the approved process. Where the review needs the original equipment or tooling manufacturer, include that requirement in the plan.

PharmaGlobalEng’s role is to discuss the tooling condition and whether an inspection, polishing or surface-engineering route is appropriate. We should not recommend surface work when the evidence instead points to a different part of the process.

When reconditioning may be worth evaluating

Reconditioning is a possible route only when the identified condition is recoverable and the tool can meet its requirements afterward. Ask what feature will be worked, how it will be checked and what would rule the tool out.

Replacing a set without investigating the mechanism may leave the same unanswered questions. Equally, keeping a damaged tool in service because replacement takes time is not a sound decision. Compare the technical suitability of each route before its cost or lead time.

A useful request: “Please review whether the observed tooling condition could justify reconditioning, and identify the measurements needed before a decision.” This asks for evidence and scope rather than a guarantee that polishing will cure the defect.

Keep designed layers separate from unintended fractures

For a multilayer product, label the intended layer sequence, the position of the separation and which material was present on each side. Compare the first-layer and final-compression records rather than filing only one main-force value. Record whether the separation occurs at the designed interface or within one layer; those observations lead to different questions for the development team.

For a single-layer product, preserve a side view showing the number and location of fracture planes. Keep the sampling stage with each image so a later reviewer can distinguish newly observed damage from samples deliberately broken for examination. If tablet mass or output changed during a trial, include the weight data and compression-time comparison. The additional records help avoid attributing an improvement to precompression when the trial changed several conditions.

Document the fracture plane and the condition of the sample

Use a side view to show where a separation lies relative to the upper face, lower face and tablet band. Keep the orientation with the sample record. If several planes are visible, record each rather than describing every tablet as one generic split. Include an intact comparison tablet so the reviewer can relate the fracture to the intended shape and thickness.

Identify whether the sample separated during production, during later handling or during a deliberate laboratory examination. These are different observations. A fracture exposed by a test can contribute evidence, but it should not be counted silently as a spontaneously laminated tablet. Record the method used to reveal an internal condition and the limitations of what that method can establish.

Preserve the sampling point and elapsed time before examination. If pieces were found loose together, do not assume they came from the same tablet unless that identity can be established. Keep uncertain associations marked as uncertain. The objective is a reliable description of the split and its history, giving the formulation and tooling reviewers something more specific than an unlabelled photograph of broken material.

Example: separation in a deliberately layered tablet

For an illustrative bilayer investigation, first distinguish separation at the intended interface from a fracture within either layer. Keep the layer identities, sequence and corresponding production records together. A general report of “lamination” does not tell the reviewer whether the interface or the integrity of one layer is the principal question.

Compare the first-layer and final-compression histories for the affected and acceptable periods. Identify changes in material lots, layer-weight observations, setup and timing without assuming that one is responsible. Where the available machine records combine information from several events, ask the equipment specialist what can be separated reliably. Do not borrow a single-layer setting or a research-study value as a remedy for the layered product.

A proposed trial should state which separation pattern it addresses and how that pattern will be evaluated. Keep the other required tablet attributes in the plan, including the product-specific requirements associated with the layers. A change that leaves the interface intact but introduces cracking inside one layer has changed the failure mode rather than necessarily resolving the product problem. Report those outcomes separately so the next decision remains tied to the actual evidence.

Compare competing explanations rather than collecting unrelated data

Prepare a short investigation note for each plausible explanation. State the observation supporting it, the information that is missing and the next comparison that could challenge it. An air-related explanation may lead to review of the compression sequence, while a finding that follows a specific die or station may require closer physical and equipment assessment. Neither route should be selected solely because it is easiest to change.

Consider evidence that does not fit. If a proposed setup change affects the recorded profile but the same fracture remains, retain that result. If a new set appears successful but the batch and speed also changed, record the combined change rather than claiming the old set caused the defect. A useful review makes contradictory observations visible and uses them to refine the next question.

Separate absence of evidence from evidence of absence. A missing trace does not show that unloading was normal, and a brief inspection with no visible split does not prove that the tablet was internally sound throughout later processing. Identify what the available method can actually detect. Where a decision requires a more specialized examination, specify its purpose before sending samples so the resulting report answers the relevant question.

Verify the stages where the split originally became apparent

If lamination first appeared during transfer, coating or later handling, include that stage in the verification plan. Retain samples from appropriate points before and after it using the same definitions as the baseline. A clean sample at press discharge alone does not answer a complaint that was historically observed only after another operation.

Keep the timing of observations and the conditions of intermediate storage. Where the trial omits a later stage, state that the outcome is provisional for that part of the product route. Avoid substituting a harsher improvised handling test without establishing what it measures; a different test may create a new fracture pattern that is unrelated to the original observation. Let the responsible laboratory define a suitable assessment.

Close the investigation with separate results for each fracture pattern and relevant stage. Record the amount examined, the observed failures and any limitations in detection. Include the tooling and material identities so a future campaign can reproduce the evaluated conditions. This creates a usable basis for follow-up: the next team can see whether the change addressed the original split, shifted it to another location, or still needs assessment farther along the process.

Verify the change across the relevant stages

Define the test question, baseline, sampling stages and observation period before starting. Agree what counts as lamination and how affected samples will be recorded. Include product-specific testing and any later stage where the original damage was first observed.

Record each approved change. When a tooling intervention and a process change are both necessary, explain why the trial cannot isolate their individual contributions. A successful combined trial may be useful without proving that either change would work alone.

Do not close the investigation solely because no split was visible at the press discharge during a brief check. State which stages and periods were assessed, what the observations showed and what remains uncertain. Acceptance and release decisions belong to the manufacturer’s responsible teams.

For future campaigns, retain the tooling identity, work performed, comparison record and follow-up requirements. This gives the next investigation a usable baseline instead of starting again with an unexplained replacement order.

Bring the fracture evidence to the tooling review

Send PharmaGlobalEng side-view photographs, the press and tooling details, the sampling stage and a summary of the affected run. Include the changes already attempted and their results. Contact us before shipping tools so the review scope, handling requirements and expected turnaround can be agreed.

25 practical questions and answers

Quick answers to the questions covered in this guide. Use the detailed sections above for the evidence and context behind each answer.

01

What is tablet lamination?

Lamination describes separation within the tablet body into layers. Record the fracture location, number of visible planes and sampling history before assigning a process or tooling cause.

02

How does lamination differ from capping?

Capping separates a crown from the body, while lamination involves internal layers. Keep the fracture categories distinct so the investigation and verification address the correct observed pattern.

03

Which views help document lamination?

Use side and face photographs with consistent orientation. Include an intact comparison tablet and preserve the sampling point, time and conditions under which separation was observed.

04

Does a visible split prove air entrapment?

No. Air-related behavior is one hypothesis that may require investigation. Compare the actual compression, material, tooling and timing evidence rather than diagnosing from appearance alone.

05

Does more precompression always prevent lamination?

No universal setting follows from the cited research. Review the product and equipment evidence and define an authorized trial that preserves the applicable requirements.

06

Why include unloading information?

The release portion of compression can matter to the investigation. Keep available cycle records so a profile change is not reduced to only its peak force or precompression setting.

07

What changes when the product is deliberately multilayered?

Identify whether separation occurs at the intended interface or within a layer. Preserve the layer sequence and corresponding records instead of treating every split as the same single-layer problem.

08

Which bilayer records should remain separate?

Retain first-layer and final-compression histories with layer identities and available layer-weight observations. A single general force value may not describe the events relevant to the fracture.

09

Can keeping an interface intact still leave a failed product?

Yes. A change may move cracking inside one layer or affect another requirement. Report each fracture pattern and relevant product result rather than declaring success from the interface alone.

10

How should deliberately broken samples be identified?

Label the examination method and distinguish those samples from spontaneous production failures. A revealed fracture can supply evidence without being counted silently as naturally occurring lamination.

11

Can loose fragments be assumed to belong to one tablet?

Only when identity can be established. Keep uncertain associations labeled as uncertain so the fracture description does not rely on pieces that may come from different tablets.

12

What is a suitable lamination baseline?

Prefer an acceptable run of the same product and tooling design. Document differences in material, equipment, sampling or testing so the comparison does not imply equivalence that was not established.

13

What should a lamination comparison record contain?

Keep fracture timing and location, compression history, speed, batch processing and tooling specification. Retain original observations and measurement methods instead of only a conclusion that conditions were normal.

14

Does a tapered die automatically resolve lamination?

No. Published results depend on formulation and configuration. Any geometry proposal needs the relevant specification, compatibility review and product-specific evaluation rather than a universal die-taper recommendation.

15

Can punch–die clearance be changed from a web recommendation?

Do not improvise a clearance or penetration change. Obtain the applicable design and equipment review, including dimensions, compatibility and the process requirements controlling any proposed trial.

16

When is reconditioning worth assessing?

Consider it when a recoverable physical condition is identified and required geometry and integrity can remain acceptable. The proposal should specify the work and how acceptance will be verified.

17

Does replacing a tooling set prove the old set caused lamination?

No. If material or setup also changed, the result applies to the combination. Compare measured differences and trial conditions before assigning the improvement to tooling alone.

18

How should competing explanations be compared?

For each hypothesis, state supporting observations, missing information and a comparison that could challenge it. Use contradictory results to refine the next question instead of hiding them.

19

Does a missing force trace show unloading was normal?

No. Missing information remains a limitation. Identify what the available method can establish and which further measurement is necessary for a decision about the cycle.

20

Can a surface photograph confirm internal integrity?

Not for every internal condition. The responsible laboratory should define any additional examination needed, with the method and its detection limits retained in the investigation.

21

Why verify later processing stages?

If separation originally became apparent after transfer, coating or handling, a clean discharge sample is incomplete evidence. Include appropriate comparisons around the stage where the historical problem appeared.

22

Can an improvised harsh handling test replace the original observation?

Not without establishing what it measures. A different test may create another fracture pattern; use a suitable assessment defined by the responsible laboratory for the investigation question.

23

How should several simultaneous changes be reported?

State that the trial assessed their combined effect and identify the limits of attribution. A successful result does not prove that either individual change would work alone.

24

What belongs in the lamination closeout?

Retain the original split pattern, evidence, approved changes, sample counts, stages examined and remaining limits. Keep material and tooling identities so later campaigns can reproduce the evaluated conditions.

25

What should accompany a lamination inquiry?

Send side-view fracture photographs, sampling history, press and tooling details, material and layer information where relevant, and previous trial results. Explain which inspection or decision the review should support.
