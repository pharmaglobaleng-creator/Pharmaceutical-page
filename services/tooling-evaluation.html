What is tooling performance evaluation?

Tooling performance evaluation organizes photographs, measurements, defect patterns, station identity, tooling history, coating condition, cleaning, formulation, and press data before corrective action. The correct approach is application-specific and should be based on documented tooling condition, process evidence, product requirements, and risk.

Engineering Facts Library

Tooling Performance Evaluation Facts and Answers

1

Tooling evaluation begins with a clear defect definition.

Fact: Tooling evaluation begins with a clear defect definition.

Answer: Sticking, picking, filming, corrosion, wear, chipping, capping, and poor release are different observations. The investigation should define what is visible before selecting measurements or corrective action.
Engineering action

Use consistent defect terminology.

2

Photographs are useful but not always conclusive.

Fact: Photographs are useful but not always conclusive.

Answer: High-quality images can reveal location, pattern, engraving, coating damage, and buildup, but they may not show crack depth, dimensions, or subsurface damage.
Engineering action

Use photos for triage and physical inspection for final disposition.

3

Defect timing is diagnostic evidence.

Fact: Defect timing is diagnostic evidence.

Answer: A problem that appears immediately differs from one that develops after heat, humidity exposure, or a long run.
Engineering action

Record elapsed time and tablets produced.

4

Station mapping separates local and systemic causes.

Fact: Station mapping separates local and systemic causes.

Answer: If only specific stations are affected, tooling identity, alignment, guides, lubrication, or local machine condition may be involved.
Engineering action

Track defects by station.

5

Upper and lower punches should be evaluated separately.

Fact: Upper and lower punches should be evaluated separately.

Answer: They may experience different contact, temperature, gravity, and product-transfer conditions.
Engineering action

Record which punch face is affected.

6

Tooling history improves diagnosis.

Fact: Tooling history improves diagnosis.

Answer: Age, production volume, restoration cycles, coating history, cleaning, storage, and prior defects provide context.
Engineering action

Maintain a lifecycle file.

7

Formulation history belongs in a tooling evaluation.

Fact: Formulation history belongs in a tooling evaluation.

Answer: Moisture, binder, lubricant, glidant, granulation, particle size, fines, and batch changes can drive visible tooling symptoms.
Engineering action

Include batch and process data.

8

Press conditions should be recorded together.

Fact: Press conditions should be recorded together.

Answer: Compression force, precompression, dwell time, speed, feeder settings, temperature, and run duration interact.
Engineering action

Avoid evaluating one setting alone.

9

Cleaning methods can create or hide damage.

Fact: Cleaning methods can create or hide damage.

Answer: Residue can mask defects, while abrasive or incompatible cleaning can create scratches and coating loss.
Engineering action

Document chemistry, tools, time, and drying.

10

Visual inspection requires controlled lighting.

Fact: Visual inspection requires controlled lighting.

Answer: Directional and diffuse lighting reveal different defects, and magnification helps assess pits, scratches, edges, and coating damage.
Engineering action

Standardize inspection conditions.

11

Dimensional inspection should be risk-based.

Fact: Dimensional inspection should be risk-based.

Answer: Measure features that affect fit, tablet geometry, strength, and restoration limits rather than collecting numbers without purpose.
Engineering action

Tie measurements to function.

12

Engraving should be inspected as geometry.

Fact: Engraving should be inspected as geometry.

Answer: Depth, stroke width, islands, corners, spacing, wear, and roughness influence legibility and picking.
Engineering action

Use feature-specific inspection.

13

Cup geometry affects tablet performance.

Fact: Cup geometry affects tablet performance.

Answer: Depth, radii, transitions, land, and edges influence compaction and release.
Engineering action

Compare actual geometry with approved design.

14

Die condition affects ejection and consistency.

Fact: Die condition affects ejection and consistency.

Answer: Bore wear, corrosion, roughness, entry condition, and dimensions can influence fill, compaction, and ejection.
Engineering action

Inspect punches and dies together.

15

Coating identity must be confirmed.

Fact: Coating identity must be confirmed.

Answer: Trade names or color alone may not accurately identify the coating family or variant.
Engineering action

Use supplier documentation.

16

Coating failure location helps identify mechanism.

Fact: Coating failure location helps identify mechanism.

Answer: Edge chipping, face wear, engraving damage, and generalized loss can indicate different causes.
Engineering action

Map the failure pattern.

17

Corrosion requires chemistry review.

Fact: Corrosion requires chemistry review.

Answer: Product, cleaning agents, water, drying, storage, and substrate exposure all influence corrosion risk.
Engineering action

Investigate the environment.

18

Wear should be classified by mechanism.

Fact: Wear should be classified by mechanism.

Answer: Abrasive, adhesive, impact, fretting, and corrosive wear require different responses.
Engineering action

Name the mechanism before recommending treatment.

Fact: Surface roughness data needs context.

Answer: Ra values without location, cutoff, direction, and defect information can mislead.

Document measurement conditions.

Answer: Tooling sets can contain station-to-station variation, mixed service history, and localized damage.

Use representative sampling.

Mistakes to Avoid

Common Engineering Mistakes

Selecting a treatment before defining the defectPolishing, coating, or replacement should follow evidence rather than assumption.
Changing multiple variables at onceUncontrolled trials make it difficult to identify the true cause.

Frequently Asked Questions

Tooling Performance Evaluation FAQ

What is tooling performance evaluation?

It is an engineering evaluation and service topic that should be considered within the complete tooling, formulation, process, cleaning, and operating system.

When should a manufacturer request tooling performance evaluation?

A review is useful when defects repeat, tooling condition is uncertain, restoration or coating decisions are needed, or production performance changes.

Can photographs support a preliminary review?

Yes. Clear photographs can support triage, but physical inspection or additional data may be needed for final recommendations.

Does PharmaGlobalEng support international manufacturers?

Yes. Preliminary technical review and project coordination can be performed worldwide, subject to scope, shipping, documentation, customs, and service availability.

What information should be submitted?

Provide tooling and tablet photos, defect location and timing, tooling material and coating, cleaning history, process conditions, formulation observations, and previous corrective actions.

Are recommendations application-specific?

Yes. The same treatment may perform differently with different products, tools, presses, cleaning methods, and environments.

Can surface work replace process troubleshooting?

Not always. Moisture, lubrication, heat, force, dwell time, speed, and formulation behavior may remain primary causes.

How are restore-versus-replace decisions made?

The decision should compare integrity, dimensions, geometry, damage, risk, remaining life, and production requirements.

Can coated tooling be evaluated?

Yes. Coating identity, condition, edges, substrate, cleaning, wear, and damage mechanism should be reviewed.

Who approves return to service?

The manufacturer remains responsible for product, process, quality, validation, and return-to-service approval.

Why are lifecycle records useful?

They show wear, restoration, coating, cleaning, and failure history across multiple campaigns.

Can the same acceptance criteria be used for every application?

Not necessarily. Risk, product behavior, tooling design, and production requirements may justify different criteria.

How should findings be documented?

Use traceable tooling identity, photographs, measurements, observations, work performed, limitations, and final disposition.

Can recurring defects indicate a system problem?

Yes. Repeated damage may involve handling, storage, cleaning, press condition, formulation, or environment.

What is the first step?

Start with a precise problem statement and collect representative evidence before selecting a solution.
